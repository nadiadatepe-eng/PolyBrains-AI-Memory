Research plan · draft for discussion · updated 2026-08-19 PolyBrains

Thousand Brains Theory as the substrate. Polymath properties as the specification. The question is whether a system of many complete models in many reference frames can be made to disagree productively — and whether that is measurable.

Where we are, end of 2026-08-19 — four experiments in one day P8P9P10P11

The morning's finding: this project's central claim, "voting harms out-of-distribution accuracy", is withdrawn as a general result. Re-scoring the same 2000 episodes under three read-outs moves the effect from −2.80 pp to +30.20 pp. The sign is set by the scoring criterion, not by voting.

The afternoon tested what happens when you make modules exchange votes more. P9: iterating the vote buys agreement and pays for it in independence. P10: Nadi's parliamentary design — propose, then two rounds of devil's-advocate opposition — cuts confident errors, but the opposition itself contributed nothing. P11: rerun under a rule that can carry dissent; the opposition becomes measurably active and still does not improve accuracy.

The conclusion after five experiments: across every manipulation we tried, whether and how often modules exchange votes moves results by tens of percentage points, while which arithmetic reduces those votes moves almost nothing. That is now confirmed three independent ways.

P12 then tested H2 for the first time and refuted it — in-domain-confidence weighting improves multi-frame agreement rather than capturing it, because the weight is bounded by the polymath specification's own "never silence a module" invariant. A third pattern also appeared twice in unrelated mechanisms: both iterated voting (P9) and vote weighting (P12) trade a couple of points of any accuracy for large gains in unanimity.

The conclusion, stated plainly

Three findings are solid enough to defend, and one attractive story is not supported.

1. The criterion owns the result
strongest		 On identical episodes, voting looks harmful (−2.80 pp), neutral (+0.20 pp) or transformative (+30.20 pp, t=−14.40) depending only on how you score an episode. Verified non-circular on a threshold-free metric (+0.564 modules/episode, t=15.11) and reproduced in a second engine (DuckDB SQL sharing no code with the Python).
2. More exchange buys agreement, not truth
replicated		 Three plain rounds take unanimity 43.50 → 79.00% while any accuracy falls 96.50 → 90.50% and confident errors quadruple 1.50 → 8.00%. Modules converge on each other rather than on the object.
3. Structure resists false consensus
replicated twice		 Staged, few-speakers-per-exchange voting holds confident errors at 1.00–2.50% where plain iteration reaches 8.00% at the same exchange count — under max (−5.50 pp, t=−3.77) and again under consensus (−7.00 pp, t=−3.50). This is Nadi's design idea, and it works.
4. The adversarial content
not supported		 The devil's advocate is measurably active under consensus — it cuts agreement by 18.00 pp (t=−3.06) — but does not convert that friction into accuracy (errors t=−1.50, any t=0.88). What works is the staging, not the opposition.

What we corrected about ourselves today. The first P11 write-up said the opposition was "inert under max" and freed by consensus. Under max the same contrast was already −14.00 pp (t=−2.24) — a near-miss, not an absence. The claim compares two contrasts, so it needs the difference-in-differences: −4.00 pp, t(4)=−0.60, n.s. The mechanism story is consistent with the data and not confirmed by it.

A correction to our own earlier record

P7 reported that voting creates correlated failure: all-fail episodes rose from 1 to 15. That number is correct and reproduces. But voting simultaneously moved 261 three-correct episodes up to four and five correct, which the earlier report did not say. Reporting the tail without the bulk was one-sided. The same mechanism produces both, and the net on mean correctness is strongly positive.

In-domain confidence weighting was predicted to capture the consensus and destroy the OOD advantage. It does the opposite: +21.50 pp on unanimity (t=5.00), with no capture signature (weight spread marginally lower, t=−0.58), and the gain survives the threshold-free metric at +0.365 modules correct per episode (t=3.61) so it is not an agreement artefact.

One confound, found by re-checking and disclosed: the rotation schedule is blocked, not interleaved, so the indomain arm enters the measured OOD phase with a fully trained weight while ood trains during it. That invalidates any ood-vs-indomain accuracy ranking (never a registered prediction), and it strengthens the refutation: the blocked order gives H2 its most favourable setup and the predicted harm still does not appear.

Why it fails is the interesting part. AdaptiveVoteWeight is bounded (0.1–2.0) with an EMA, so no module can run away with the vote — a 1.49x spread is not capture. H2's failure mode needs an unbounded weight. The polymath specification's own "never silence a module, always allow recovery" invariant — frozen at CP-4 before any accuracy was seen — is what blocks it. A constraint written for ethical reasons turns out to prevent the predicted computational failure.

1. The claim we are willing to be wrong about

The failure mode of this genre is a paper that maps cognitive-science vocabulary onto a neuroscience theory and stops. That is unfalsifiable, and reviewers know it. So the project commits up front to one narrow claim that could lose.

Primary hypothesis (H1)

A voting system whose modules each hold a complete model in its own reference frame transfers to out-of-distribution inputs better than an otherwise-identical system with a single shared frame — and the size of that advantage scales with how much the modules disagree during inference, not with how many modules there are.

The second clause is what makes it losable. If the advantage tracks module count rather than module disagreement, H1 is false and we report that. It also gives an immediate control: add modules that are near-copies of each other. Under H1 they buy nothing.

Secondary hypothesis (H2) — the manipulation clause

Weighting votes by each module's in-domain confidence destroys the OOD advantage. A single high-confidence module can capture the consensus, which is the computational form of "harder to manipulate through a single narrative" failing.

H2 is the one we expect to be most informative, because Monty's current vote rule is already close to the failure case, as §3 shows.

A limit on what any of this measures — Nadi, 2026-08-20

A consensus does not need to be true. Given a question whose alternatives are all wrong and no option to abstain, agreeing is the rational move: dissent costs something and buys nothing. A brain can accept a false premise as truth when the situation requires it, and the answer that fits the moment is the one it takes — not the one that is 100% right.

This project could not see that, by construction. Every experiment before P14 evaluated objects that were in the pretrained set: train_distinctobj_predefined.yaml and eval_distinctobj_random.yaml carry the identical 10-object list. The correct answer was always inside the hypothesis space, so "does agreement imply correctness" was only ever asked under the one condition that flatters it. Every metric here scores against a known target, and a system that only measures truth-tracking cannot observe convergence that is not about truth.

P14 and P15 tested it and it holds. With the target absent from graph memory: 90.00% unanimity at 0.00% accuracy (P14), and across four held-out objects a range of 20% to 100% unanimity, all of it wrong (P15). Most of that agreement is present with the vote path disabled — so the mechanism is not inspectors deferring to each other but a shared perceptual prior: modules trained alike map an unseen object to the same nearest neighbour. The agreement is determined before any vote is cast. Reasoning: reports/reconsideration-consensus.md.

What this does to H1 — the read-out, not the hypothesis

H1 is not refuted. What P14/P15 compromise is the read-out: any unanimity figure in this project measures correctness plus a similarity constant, and nothing so far separates the two. P8's headline — voting lifting unanimity from 10.80% to 41.00% — was read as agreement about a correct answer; ~85% unanimity is reachable here with no voting and no correct answer available at all.

H1's second clause needs restating. It scales the advantage with module disagreement. P15 shows disagreement is largely a function of how novel the input is — a property of the input, not of the modules. Unanimity was 100% for strawberry (very close to a trained object) and 20% for mug (a handle no trained object shares).

The publishable relation: false consensus is highest exactly where the unknown resembles the known, and lowest where the input is genuinely novel. A system agreeing confidently is evidence that the input looked familiar, not that the answer is right.

P16 closes the obvious escape. Weighting by confidence does not separate true from false consensus: the most confidently held answer in the sweep is a wrong one, significantly more confident than the correct case. So agreement, confidence and correctness are three different things, and in this substrate the first two are uninformative about the third. Every experiment before P14 measured only agreement and read it as evidence about correctness.

What we are explicitly not claiming

  • Not that cortical columns and human polymaths are the same mechanism. The analogy motivates the design; it is not evidence for it.
  • Not that Monty is a model of a polymath. It is a testbed with the right shape.
  • Not that the polymath list of properties is validated psychology. It is our design brief, sourced from a practitioner's intuition, and treated as such.

3. The gap — verified at the pinned sha CP-1 confirmed

We read Monty's voting implementation rather than its documentation, then re-verified every claim against the pinned sha with an executable probe (tests/cp1_probe_vote_path.py, exit 0). Full write-up: reports/vote-path.md.

There are two vote implementations, and only one is ours. graph_matching.py serves displacement/feature-matching LMs; evidence_matching/model.py serves evidence LMs. Our 5-LM configs use evidence_5lm_nn10_dod003 → EvidenceGraphLM, so the second is the path that matters. Its _combine_votes transforms votes into the receiving module's frame and concatenates them, with no per-object tallying.

How voting actually works today

  1. Each learning module computes evidence per hypothesis per object graph.
  2. send_out_vote() (learning_module.py:403) emits only hypotheses whose scaled evidence exceeds vote_evidence_threshold (default 0.8). Each vote is a Message carrying location, pose and a confidence.
  3. Which module hears which is a static lm_to_lm_vote_matrix, assigned once at monty_base.py:99 and never mutated. Our 5-LM config is all-to-all.
  4. receive_votes() → _update_evidence_with_vote() (learning_module.py:902) builds a KD-tree over incoming vote locations, takes the 3 nearest to each hypothesis, and reduces them with np.ma.max at line 938.
  5. The result is blended into the module's own evidence, weighted by a scalar vote_weight (default 1).

The three findings that define the project — all confirmed

(a) The aggregation is a max, not a consensus. np.ma.max over the vote neighbourhood means the loudest single vote in the radius wins outright. Stronger than we first thought: the variable is called distance_weighted_vote_evidence, but the weighting is commented out at line 940 and upstream's own comment at 936 says "Currently unweighted". Probe: three peers at 0.95 / 0.42 / 0.40 reduce to 0.95; deleting the two agreeing modules gives 0.95, identical. Two modules agreeing change the outcome by exactly zero. This is H2's failure, present by default.

(b) Disagreement is never represented — in the vote path. No variance, entropy or spread of incoming votes is computed, stored or transmitted. Two corrections from CP-1: (i) an np.std does exist at learning_module.py:1159, but it is the spread across object hypotheses inside one module, not across modules; (ii) a cross-module agreement mechanism does exist — AnyLMsMatch in experiment/match_criteria.py, which our config sets to count: 3. But it is a terminal condition that stops the episode, a binary count of terminal states. It never feeds back into the vote and measures no dispersion. The paper must state this distinction; a reviewer who knows Monty will check.

Updated 2026-08-19 after P8: this is no longer only a definitional footnote. AnyLMsMatch turned out to set the sign of the project's headline result — voting harms under it and helps under unanimity, a 33 pp swing on identical episodes. Anything the write-up says about the value of voting is conditional on this criterion, and the choice has to be argued rather than inherited. See reports/p8-unanimity.md.

(c) vote_weight is a static scalar. Confirmed exactly: assigned once at learning_module.py:285, read at 951 and 960, and the probe found no other assignment in any method of the class. No mechanism makes a module that is repeatedly wrong out of domain any quieter.

This is good news, not criticism. Monty is honest early-beta code and these are exactly the seams a contribution can sit in. It also means our intervention is small and legible: we do not fork the architecture, we change the vote rule.

  • Vote on State, and CMP displacements — neither overlaps.

Assessment: the contribution is open, but not virgin territory. Nobody has proposed measuring disagreement across modules and feeding it back into aggregation, and the max reduction has never been raised. The paper must cite these items and state what we add beyond them. Claiming the vote rule is unexamined would be false and checkable.

4. Theory: the polymath list, mapped honestly

Thirteen properties were given as the specification. We sort them by whether Thousand Brains Theory already explains them, because the ones it does not are the contribution.

4.3 The rival theories CP-2 done

Gentner's structure-mapping theory (1983, Cognitive Science 7:155–170) is the established formal account of "translate ideas from one field into another". Relations between objects map, attributes do not, and the selection rule is the systematicity principle: "A predicate that belongs to a mappable system of mutually interconnecting relationships is more likely to be imported into the target than is an isolated predicate." Crucially the rules are syntactic — independent of domain content.

E3 is the fork. Structure-mapping predicts near-clone modules transfer as well as diverse ones, provided each holds the same systematic relational structure — diversity per se is not its mechanism. H1 predicts clones buy nothing. If clones do as well, H1's second clause is false and the result favours systematicity.

We do not claim to refute structure-mapping. The honest position is that a reference frame may be the cortical mechanism for what Gentner described at the computational level; H1 adds a claim about disagreement that structure-mapping does not make. One limitation we state ourselves: Gentner's relations hold between propositions, while Monty's modules vote on 3D poses. Whether a pose hypothesis is a "relation" in his sense is arguable.

The ensemble-diversity literature is the more dangerous rival. Rubinstein et al. (arXiv:2409.16797) "encourage the ensemble members to disagree" on hard samples and report "large benefits from the diversification" for OOD generalisation. That is uncomfortably close to H1: induced disagreement, measured OOD benefit.

The counterweight is Ortega et al. (PMLR v151), who derive "the exact trade-off that exists between this diversity measure, the performance of the individual predictors and the generalisation error of the ensemble". Diversity is not free — members made more diverse are usually made individually worse, and the ensemble gains only while the diversity outruns that loss. This is the strongest reason to expect H1 to fail.

Three differences we must measure or drop: (i) ensemble members share one input representation and vote on labels, TBT modules hold complete models in different reference frames and vote on poses and locations; (ii) ensemble diversity is induced by a training objective, ours is a consequence of embodiment; (iii) ensembles aggregate by averaging or majority vote — no ensemble paper we found defends a max-reduction, which is exactly what CP-1 confirmed Monty does. E2 is the fork here, because ensemble theory says little about confidence-weighted aggregation under a max it never uses.

CP-2 gate met. Two experiments do discriminative work: E3 forks structure-mapping against H1, E2 forks the ensemble account against H2. Full analysis with quotations and the dishonesty checklist: reports/rivals.md.

5.2 The three measures

D-index (disagreement)

Spread of incoming vote confidences and pose hypotheses across modules at each step. Normalised 0–1. This is the independent variable H1 says the OOD advantage should track. Definition must be frozen in CP-3 before any result is looked at, or we will tune it into significance.

Capture rate

Fraction of inference steps where the consensus equals the single highest-confidence module's vote. Under np.ma.max this should be near 1 by construction — that is the sanity check that our instrumentation is correct before we change anything.

OOD delta

Accuracy on held-out perturbations minus accuracy in domain, per arm. H1 predicts multi-frame beats single-frame on the delta while being no better, possibly worse, in domain. If multi-frame simply wins everywhere, the result is less interesting and probably an artefact — we would suspect capacity, not structure.

9. Publication shape

The honest genre works only if the claim is about what happened, not about what it means. Structure follows from that.

  1. Abstract — the measured result first. Not the analogy.
  2. Motivation — TBT, the polymath specification, §4.1 clearly marked as mapping and not evidence.
  3. The gap — §3, read from source at a pinned sha. This section is a contribution on its own even if every hypothesis fails.
  4. Method — consensus rule, D-index, w(t). Small enough to state in full.
  5. Pre-registration — predictions with their commit shas, quoted.
  6. Results — including the null and negative ones, in the body.
  7. Rival accounts — structure-mapping and ensemble diversity, from CP-2.
  8. What we did not do — cut checkpoints, with reasons.

Why this can be published even if H1 and H2 both fail. Three outputs survive a total negative result:

  1. A source-level analysis of how consensus actually works in the reference implementation of Thousand Brains Theory, including that its default aggregation is winner-take-all.
  2. A working D-index and instrumentation for disagreement that Monty does not currently have, contributable upstream regardless of our findings.
  3. A pre-registered negative result on multi-frame OOD transfer, which is the kind of thing this field has too few of.

Publication framing, revised twice on 2026-08-19

The list above still holds, with one change forced by P8 and a second by P9–P11. The result is no longer "voting harms". It is:

The measured value of multi-frame voting is dominated by the episode-scoring criterion — which the reference implementation supplies as a default and which the literature does not argue for. On identical episodes the effect runs from −2.80 pp to +30.20 pp depending only on that choice.

That is a harder claim to dismiss than a negative result, and it generalises past this codebase: any multi-module benchmark scored by "did any module get it right" is measuring independence and calling it accuracy. The threshold-free metric (mean modules correct) is the defence against a reviewer who says unanimity is as arbitrary as AnyLMsMatch.

P9–P11 add a second, independent claim that shares no data with the first:

Making modules exchange votes more does not make them more right, it makes them more alike. Three plain rounds take unanimity from 43.50 to 79.00% while accuracy falls and confident errors quadruple. Staging the exchange — few speakers per round — prevents that collapse; adversarial content within the staging does not add to it.

Both claims point the same way, which is why they belong in one paper: the interesting variable is the protocol of exchange, not the arithmetic of aggregation. Three experiments independently found the reduction rule to be near-irrelevant (P8 within 1.6 pp, P10 n.s., P11 max |t|=1.63) while protocol changes moved results by 30 pp. A fourth contribution is methodological and unusually concrete: five sweeps were voided here, none caught by reading the code, all caught by pre-registered controls, liveness probes and count assertions.
